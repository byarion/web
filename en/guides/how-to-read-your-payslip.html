How to read your payslip: what each line means

The payslip is the document you receive every month with the details of your salary. To many people it looks like a maze of acronyms, but its structure is simple: what you earn, what is deducted and what you actually receive.

The general idea in three steps

  1. Accruals (devengos): everything you are entitled to be paid (your «gross» pay).
  2. Deductions: what is taken off (Social Security and income tax).
  3. Net pay (líquido a percibir): what reaches your account, that is, your «net» pay.

The formula is: accruals − deductions = net pay.

The header: company and personal details

At the top you will see your company's name, your details, your job category, your length of service and the period the payslip covers (for example, the 1st to the 30th of a month). It helps you check that everything matches your contract.

Accruals: what you earn

This is the part that adds up everything you are paid for:

  • Base salary: the fixed part of your pay, set by your contract or collective agreement.
  • Supplements: extras for seniority, position, night work, transport and so on.
  • Overtime or commissions: if there are any that month.
  • Extra payments: if your salary is spread over 14 payments, in two months of the year you will receive more. If they are «prorated», they are spread over the 12 payslips and you see them included every month.

The total of these items is the total accrued, or your gross pay for that month.

Deductions: what is taken off

There are two big blocks:

1. Social Security contributions

This is your contribution to the system that funds pensions, unemployment benefit, sick leave and other benefits. It is calculated as a percentage of your contribution base. Altogether, the employee's share is usually around 6.5%, split into several lines:

  • Common contingencies: covers things like retirement or common sickness leave.
  • Unemployment: covers unemployment benefit if you lose your job.
  • Vocational training: a small contribution towards training.
  • MEI (Intergenerational Equity Mechanism): a small additional contribution created to strengthen the pension system.

The exact percentages are updated over the years, so check the ones on your payslip or those published by Social Security.

2. Income tax withholding

This is an advance payment of income tax. Your employer calculates a percentage based on your salary, your family situation and other data, deducts it from each payslip and pays it to the tax authority on your behalf. When you file your tax return, what you have advanced is compared with what you actually owe: you may have to pay, get a refund or end up at zero.

Net pay: what you really get

This is the last number on the payslip and the one deposited in your account. It is the one that matters when planning your budget, for example with the 50/30/20 rule.

A step-by-step example

Suppose a gross salary of €24,000 a year with extra payments prorated over 12 months. The percentages in this example are only illustrative; the real ones depend on your case.

  • Total accrued: €24,000 ÷ 12 = €2,000 a month.
  • Social Security (≈ 6.5%): €2,000 × 6.5% = €130.
  • Income tax withholding (example of 12%): €2,000 × 12% = €240.
  • Total deductions: €130 + €240 = €370.
  • Net pay: €2,000 − €370 = €1,630.

To estimate your own case, try the net salary calculator.

What you do not see: the cost to the employer

On top of your gross salary, the employer pays its own share of Social Security for you, usually around 30% extra on the contribution base. That is why a worker costs quite a bit more than their gross salary, even though that part does not appear in what you receive.

What to check every month

  • That the base salary and supplements match your contract or collective agreement.
  • That overtime or commissions have been paid if you worked them.
  • That the income tax percentage makes sense for your situation (if your family situation changes, you can tell your employer).
  • That the period and days worked are correct.
  • That you keep your payslips: they serve as proof of income when applying for a loan or renting a home.

If something does not add up, ask your HR department or payroll adviser. You have the right to have it explained.

In short

Payslip = what you earn (accruals) minus what is deducted (Social Security and income tax) = what you receive (net pay). Base your budget on that last figure.
