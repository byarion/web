Renting or buying a home: how to compare

«Paying rent is throwing money away» and «buying is always better» are two very repeated phrases, and neither is always true. To decide well you need to compare the real costs of each option, including some that are not obvious at first sight.

What buying really costs

  • The down payment: usually around 20% of the price, which the bank does not finance.
  • Purchase costs: taxes (ITP or VAT and stamp duty, depending on the type of home and the region), notary, land registry, paperwork and valuation. As a rough guide, they usually add up to between 8% and 12% of the price, but it varies.
  • The mortgage payment: and remember that only the interest part is a cost; the part that repays principal is savings that becomes yours.
  • Ownership costs: property tax (IBI), building fees, home insurance and maintenance or repairs. A common approach is to set aside a percentage of the home's value each year, and not to forget a fund for the unexpected.
  • The opportunity cost: the money for the down payment and costs could be invested or saved elsewhere and earn a return.

What renting really costs

  • The monthly rent, which can rise over time according to the contract and current regulations.
  • The deposit (recovered later, but it ties up money), and sometimes other guarantees.
  • Contents insurance, optional but advisable.
  • You do not pay property tax or major repairs, which are the landlord's responsibility.

A 10-year example (simplified)

Suppose a home worth €200,000. You buy with €40,000 down, a €160,000 mortgage over 25 years at 3% (payment of about €759) and about €20,000 of initial costs. Let us see how much money is «lost» over 10 years, that is, the money you do not get back:

  • Mortgage interest over 10 years: about €40,900. (Of the payments, another €50,100 repays principal: that is not lost, it is equity.)
  • Ownership costs (property tax, building fees, insurance and maintenance), assuming about €250 a month: about €30,000.
  • Initial purchase costs: €20,000.
  • Opportunity cost of the €60,000 for the down payment and costs, assuming they could have earned 3% a year: about €18,000.

Total: about €109,000 over 10 years, that is, about €907 a month. If an equivalent home could be rented for €900 a month (with no increases), renting would cost €108,000 over the same period. Under these assumptions, the costs are almost level.

Note that this calculation is a simplification: it leaves out rent increases, changes in the home's price, inflation and taxes. But it teaches the important point: the mortgage payment is not the real cost, and comparing only «payment versus rent» leads to wrong conclusions.

Which factors weigh most in the decision

  • How long you plan to stay. Purchase costs are high, so buying usually makes sense over the medium and long term. If you expect to move in 2 or 3 years, renting usually works out better.
  • Job and personal stability. A mortgage is a commitment of decades.
  • Interest rates. With high rates, the mortgage makes buying more expensive. See the guide on fixed, variable or mixed mortgages.
  • How prices in your area evolve, which nobody can predict with certainty.
  • The relationship between purchase price and rent: in some areas buying is much more expensive than renting, and in others the difference is small.
  • Your safety cushion. Buying with all your savings in the down payment leaves you no margin for surprises. First, an emergency fund.

Advantages and limits of each option

  • Buying: stability, a payment that can be fixed, equity that grows as you repay and freedom to renovate. But it requires a large upfront outlay, less flexibility and you take on the risk that the price falls.
  • Renting: flexibility to move, less money tied up and fewer maintenance responsibilities. But you do not build equity in that home, the rent can rise and you depend on the landlord's decision when the contract ends.

A step-by-step way to decide

  1. Work out how much savings you have and whether, after the down payment and costs, you keep a cushion.
  2. Estimate the payment with the mortgage and loan calculator, using several interest rates.
  3. Add up the ownership costs and compare them with the rent of an equivalent home.
  4. Calculate the opportunity cost of the down payment.
  5. Think about how many years ahead you want to decide, and run the calculation for 5, 10 and 20 years.
  6. Check that the payment does not exceed roughly a third of your net income, with a margin in case rates rise.
In short

Do not compare «payment versus rent»: compare the real cost. When buying, only interest, costs and opportunity cost are lost; the repaid principal is yours. How long you will stay matters enormously.

Informational and educational content. Not financial advice. The figures in the example are illustrative and do not account for every factor; real costs depend on your region and your case.
