Fixed, variable or mixed mortgage: which to choose

When you take out a mortgage, one of the first decisions is the type of interest rate: fixed, variable or mixed. There is no single «right» choice for everyone. It depends on how much risk you can take and how stable your finances are.

The three types in one sentence each

  • Fixed-rate mortgage: the interest rate stays the same for the whole loan, so your payment does not change.
  • Variable-rate mortgage: the rate is a reference index (usually the Euribor) plus a fixed percentage called the spread. The payment is reviewed from time to time and can go up or down.
  • Mixed mortgage: for the first years (for example 5 or 10) the rate is fixed, and afterwards it becomes variable.

Fixed rate: always knowing what you pay

With a fixed-rate mortgage you know from day one how much you will pay every month until the end. If interest rates rise, your payment is unaffected. That peace of mind has a price: the starting rate is usually higher than a variable one, and if rates fall your payment does not fall with them (unless you renegotiate or switch mortgage, which can have costs).

Variable rate: Euribor plus spread

On a variable mortgage the rate you pay is, for example, «Euribor + 0.80%». If the Euribor is at 2.5%, you pay 3.3%. Every six or twelve months (depending on the contract) the Euribor is updated and your payment is recalculated. If the Euribor falls you pay less; if it rises you pay more.

The Euribor mostly moves according to what the European Central Bank does with interest rates (see The ECB and interest rates). Nobody knows for sure where it will be over the next twenty or thirty years.

Mixed mortgage: one calm stretch and one uncertain stretch

The mixed mortgage tries to combine the best of both worlds. For the first years you have a fixed payment, and after that period you move to a variable rate. It is useful if you expect to be more comfortable financially in a few years, but beware: when the fixed stretch ends, your payment can change noticeably.

An example with numbers

Imagine a €150,000 mortgage over 25 years. These are the approximate monthly payments depending on the interest rate (no fees or insurance):

  • At 3%: about €711 a month. In total you would pay about €63,400 in interest.
  • At 4%: about €792 a month. In total, about €87,500 in interest.
  • At 5%: about €877 a month. In total, about €113,100 in interest.

Going from 3% to 5% means paying around €166 more a month for the same home. With a fixed mortgage that jump does not exist; with a variable one, it can happen. You can try your own numbers in the mortgage and loan calculator.

What to look at before deciding

  • How stable your income is. If your salary is very stable and you have room, you can take some variability. If every euro counts, the certainty of a fixed payment can be worth more.
  • Safety margin. A common rule of thumb is not to spend more than about a third of your net income on the mortgage. Ask yourself: «Could I still pay if the payment went up by €150 or €200 a month?».
  • Term. The longer the term, the longer you are exposed to rate changes, and the more interest you pay in total.
  • TAE, not just TIN. Compare using the TAE (APR), which includes fees and other costs of the product.
  • Linked products. Many banks offer a lower rate if you take insurance, receive your salary there or use their cards. Work out whether those products cost you more than you save in interest.

Fees for paying off early

If in the future you want to repay part of the mortgage early, or move it to another bank, there may be a fee. Since the 2019 mortgage law these fees have maximum limits, which are lower for variable mortgages than for fixed ones. The exact percentages and periods are in your offer, so check them before signing.

The FEIN sheet and time to think

The bank must give you a sheet called the FEIN (European Standardised Information Sheet) with the terms of the offer: rate, payment, TAE, fees, linked insurance and what happens if rates change. It is designed so you can compare offers from different banks in the same format. The rules also provide for a waiting period (generally at least ten days) between receiving the paperwork and signing before a notary. Use it: do not sign in a hurry.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing only for the lowest starting payment, without thinking about what happens if rates rise.
  • Comparing only the TIN and forgetting fees and linked products.
  • Not keeping an emergency fund after buying: the emergency fund is the cushion that lets you handle a surprise without running out of savings.
  • Believing that «fixed» is always better or that «variable» is always cheaper. Neither statement is true in every case.
In short

Fixed gives certainty and usually starts at a higher rate; variable starts lower but can rise; mixed combines both. Choose according to your financial margin, not just the starting payment.
