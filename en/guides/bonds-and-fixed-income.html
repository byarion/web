What bonds and fixed income are

When you buy a bond, you are actually lending money to a government or a company. In exchange, that entity promises to pay you interest and to give your money back on a set date. This kind of investment is called fixed income.

The basic idea with an example

Imagine a €1,000 bond that pays 3% a year and matures in 5 years:

  • Every year you receive €30 in interest (the «coupon»).
  • At maturity, you get the €1,000 back (the «face value»).
  • Total over 5 years: €150 in interest, plus your capital returned.

If the issuer keeps its promise, you know in advance what you will receive. That is why it is called «fixed income».

The key words

  • Issuer: whoever borrows the money (a government, a bank, a company).
  • Face value: the amount repaid at the end.
  • Coupon: the periodic interest.
  • Maturity: the date when the face value is returned to you.
  • Yield: what you really earn per year, taking into account what you paid and what you receive.

Common types of fixed income

  • Treasury bills (Letras del Tesoro): short-term government debt (up to 12 months). They pay no coupon: you buy them for less than the face value and at maturity you receive the full face value, and that difference is your gain.
  • Government bonds: medium- and long-term public debt, with periodic coupons.
  • Corporate bonds: debt issued by companies. They usually pay more than governments, because they carry more risk.

Why a bond's price goes up and down

A bond can be sold before maturity, and then its price depends on current interest rates (which in turn depend a lot on what the ECB does).

Example: you hold a bond that pays 3%. If rates rise and new bonds pay 5%, nobody will buy yours at the same price, because it offers less. To sell it you would have to lower the price. The other way round, if rates fall, your 3% bond becomes more attractive and its price rises.

The general rule: when rates rise, bond prices fall, and vice versa. If you hold the bond until maturity, these swings do not affect you: you receive the agreed face value (as long as the issuer pays).

The risks of fixed income

  • Interest rate risk: the one we just saw. It affects long-maturity bonds more.
  • Credit risk: that the issuer cannot pay. It is lower for solid governments and higher for heavily indebted companies. The higher the interest offered, the more risk usually lies behind it.
  • Inflation risk: if prices rise faster than your return, your money loses purchasing power. See what inflation is.
  • Liquidity: sometimes a thinly traded bond is hard to sell without losing money.

Fixed income versus deposits

A deposit is similar: you lend money to a bank in exchange for interest, but it is meant to be held until the end, and there is legal protection for bank deposits up to a certain limit. Bonds can be sold on the market and their price can rise or fall, with different risks and without that same protection.

How to invest in fixed income

  • Buying bonds or bills directly through your bank or broker.
  • With fixed income funds or bond ETFs: they pool many bonds into a single product, which spreads the risk. See the index funds guide to understand how funds work in general.

Who it may make sense for

Fixed income is often used to add stability to a portfolio, or for money that will be needed in a few years. But it is not «risk-free» and it does not guarantee beating inflation. Each person should weigh their time horizon, risk tolerance and situation.

In short

A bond is a loan: you collect coupons and get the face value back at maturity. Its price falls when rates rise, and there are credit, inflation and liquidity risks.
