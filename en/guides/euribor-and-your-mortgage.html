How the Euribor affects your mortgage

If you have (or plan to take) a variable-rate mortgage, the Euribor decides how much you pay each month. Here is what happens to your payment, with numbers, when the Euribor rises or falls.

The relationship in one line

On a variable-rate mortgage, the interest rate is Euribor + spread. The bank sets the spread and it does not change; the Euribor is updated at each review. If the Euribor rises, your rate and your payment rise. If it falls, they fall.

When the payment is reviewed

Usually the review happens every 12 months (sometimes every 6). Between reviews your payment stays the same even though the Euribor moves every day. At the review, the bank takes the Euribor on the date set in your contract, adds the spread and recalculates the payment on the capital still owed and the remaining term.

Example: €150,000 over 25 years, 1% spread

Between a Euribor of 1% and 5%, the payment goes from about €636 to about €966 a month. That is why variable rates bring more uncertainty.

What happens at the first review

Imagine you start with a Euribor of 3% (4% total rate): the initial payment is about €792. After 12 months you still owe about €146,434 with 24 years to go.

  • If the Euribor rises to 4% (5% total), the payment would become about €874.
  • If the Euribor falls to 2% (3% total), the payment would become about €714.

These are indicative figures: they exclude insurance, fees and discounts.

What if I have a fixed or mixed mortgage

With a fixed mortgage, the Euribor does not affect your payment. With a mixed one, it only matters once the fixed period ends. Compare them in the guide to fixed, variable or mixed mortgages.

How to prepare for a rise

  • Calculate your payment at a rate 1 or 2 points higher and check you could still manage.
  • Keep an emergency fund.
  • Ask your bank whether you can switch to a fixed rate and what fee that would carry.

Try your own numbers in the mortgage calculator.

Summary

Variable = Euribor + spread. The payment is recalculated at each review (usually every 12 months), so a rise in the Euribor makes it more expensive and a fall makes it cheaper.

Educational information only. Not financial advice. Real terms depend on each bank and your contract.
