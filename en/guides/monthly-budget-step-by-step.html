How to make a monthly budget step by step

A budget is not a list of prohibitions: it is a plan to decide where your money goes, instead of wondering at the end of the month where it went. You can do it with pen and paper, a spreadsheet or an app.

Why it is worth it

  • It shows you how much you really spend, which is almost always different from what you think.
  • It lets you save on purpose, not just «with whatever is left».
  • It reduces stress: you know what you can spend without worrying.
  • It is the first step before building an emergency fund or starting to invest.

Step 1: Write down your net income

Note what you actually receive each month, after taxes. If your salary is fixed, that is easy. If it varies (self-employed, commissions), use the lowest month of the last six as a reference, or a cautious yearly average.

Step 2: List your fixed expenses

These repeat every month in roughly the same amount: rent or mortgage, building fees, utilities, internet and phone, insurance, transport, loan payments and subscriptions.

Step 3: Estimate your variable expenses

These change every month: food, leisure, clothes, going out, gifts. To avoid inventing numbers, check the last two or three months of bank and card statements. This is the most revealing step.

Also look out for «small leaks»: little daily purchases (coffees, snacks, apps) that seem insignificant but add up to a lot by the end of the month.

Step 4: Do not forget yearly expenses

Some expenses are paid only once or twice a year, and that is why they unbalance a particular month: car insurance, property tax, holidays, Christmas gifts. The solution is to divide them by 12 and set that amount aside every month. For example, €480 a year of insurance is €40 a month.

Step 5: Save first, spend later

This is the rule that changes results the most. Instead of saving whatever is left, decide on an amount to save and set it aside right after payday, for example with an automatic transfer to another account. If you cannot reach the 20% of the 50/30/20 rule, start with 5% or 10% and raise it over time.

Step 6: Review once a month

Spend about 15 or 20 minutes at the end of each month comparing what you planned with what you spent. The goal is not to blame yourself: it is to adjust. If a category is always over, perhaps your forecast was unrealistic and needs changing, or perhaps there is something to cut.

A complete example

Imagine a net income of €1,800 a month. One possible split:

  • Housing (rent and building fees): €600.
  • Food: €250.
  • Transport: €100.
  • Utilities, internet and phone: €120.
  • Insurance and yearly expenses (spread out): €80.
  • Leisure and going out: €200.
  • Unexpected and other variable expenses: €150.
  • Saving: €300.

Total: 600 + 250 + 100 + 120 + 80 + 200 + 150 + 300 = €1,800. Every euro has a destination, including savings. With €300 a month you can see how much you would have in a few years using the savings calculator, and how compound interest multiplies the effect of time.

Common mistakes

  • Making a budget that is too strict. If you leave no money for leisure, you will give it up within weeks. Always include a line for enjoying yourself.
  • Ignoring the unexpected. There is always something: a repair, a gift. Set aside a small amount.
  • Giving up after one bad month. A month that does not add up does not mean the method fails; it means you need to adjust.
  • Not setting savings aside right after payday. What is not set aside usually gets spent.

Tools to do it

  • A spreadsheet (Excel, Google Sheets): one column for planned and another for actual.
  • Spending apps or your bank's own app, which often categorises your transactions.
  • The envelope method: you assign an amount in cash or in an account to each variable category, and when it runs out, it runs out.
In short

Write down income and expenses, include yearly ones divided by 12, set savings aside right after payday and review once a month. A realistic budget, with room to enjoy yourself, is the one you keep.
