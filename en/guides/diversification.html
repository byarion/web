Diversification: why not to put all your eggs in one basket

«Don't put all your eggs in one basket» is one of the most repeated pieces of advice in investing. It is called diversification, and it means spreading your money so that a bad result in one single investment does not ruin the whole.

The idea with an example

Imagine you have €10,000 and put all of it in the shares of a single company. If it does badly, or goes bankrupt, you lose a lot or everything. Now imagine you spread that money across hundreds of companies in different sectors and countries. If one falls 50%, the effect on the whole is small, and others may offset it.

Diversifying does not remove risk, nor does it guarantee gains, but it reduces the chance that a single failure hurts you badly.

Why it works

Investments do not all rise and fall at the same time or by the same amount. When some do badly, others may do better or fall less. When you combine them, the path of the whole is generally smoother than that of each piece on its own.

The five ways to diversify

  • By company: not depending on a single firm. A simple way is an index fund or an ETF, which holds hundreds or thousands of companies.
  • By sector: technology, health, energy, banking, consumer goods… If all your money is in one sector, a bad year for that sector hits you entirely.
  • By country and currency: investing only in your own country makes you depend on its economy. Spreading across regions reduces that dependence.
  • By type of asset: shares, fixed income (bonds), cash, property… Each behaves differently.
  • By time: instead of investing everything at once, doing it through regular contributions, for example every month. That way you do not depend on having picked exactly the best or worst moment.

A simple example with numbers

Suppose two people have €10,000 and there is a bad year in which one company loses 60%:

  • Person A has everything in that company: loses €6,000, or 60%.
  • Person B spreads the money over 100 companies, €100 in each, and only one of them is that company: loses €60, or 0.6% of the total.

It is an extreme, simplified example, but it shows the logic: the smaller the weight of each piece, the less damage its fall does.

False diversification: the most common mistake

Having many products is not the same as being diversified. Some examples:

  • Five US technology funds are still concentrated in the same thing.
  • Buying many shares in the same sector exposes you to the same risk.
  • Having several accounts at the same bank does not diversify the bank's risk.

What matters is that your investments are truly different from each other, not that there are many of them.

What diversification does not do

  • It does not protect against general falls. In a big crisis, almost everything falls at once for a while (known as «market risk»). See the guide on what a recession is.
  • It does not guarantee returns. You can be very diversified and still lose money in a bad period.
  • It can reduce big gains. If one investment soars, holding only a part of it means you earn less than someone who bet everything on it… and also risked everything.

Too much diversification

There can also be too much of it: filling up on products that cost fees, are hard to follow and add nothing different. A couple of well-chosen, low-fee products can provide broad diversification.

Reviewing from time to time

Over time, some investments grow more than others and the split changes. Once a year, for example, you can check whether the split is still the one you wanted and adjust it if you have drifted far. This is called rebalancing the portfolio.

First things first

Before investing, it is wise to have an emergency fund, so you are not forced to sell investments at a bad moment. And to see how time and compound interest work in your favour, try the compound interest calculator.

In short

Diversifying means spreading risk across companies, sectors, countries, asset types and time. It reduces the risk of one specific failure, but it does not remove general falls or guarantee gains.
